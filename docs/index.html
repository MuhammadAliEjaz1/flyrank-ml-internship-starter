Search Content Research · Machine Learning Internship, FlyRank AI

When should a page get a second look?

We asked whether a small learned model could rank declining search pages for editorial review better than a hand-written rule. On held-out clients, a shallow decision tree averaged roughly twice the rule's precision at the top of the queue — but its advantage swung widely by which clients were tested, and it flagged an entirely different set of pages than the rule did. The honest result is a two-signal playbook, not a single verdict.

By Muhammad Ali Ejaz Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring Data: FlyRank internship warehouse, Jan–Jun 2026

01Introduction

This project grew out of a real FlyRank workflow problem: content teams already use hand-written optimization flags — Healthy, Fix CTR, Fix Content, Zombie Page — to triage a portfolio too large to review by hand. Those flags are useful triage cues, but they're rules, not learned patterns, and nobody had checked whether a simple learned model could sharpen that first pass. This project is that check, applied to one slice of the problem: which declining pages should a content editor look at first, this week, given limited review time?

The unit of analysis is one page's monthly performance snapshot. The output is a ranked queue with a reason code attached to each entry, so a reviewer can see why a page was flagged, not just that it was. A wrong call has an asymmetric cost: flagging a healthy page wastes an editor's time, but missing a genuinely declining, visible page lets it lose ground silently — the more expensive mistake of the two. This paper is a case study of that problem, worked end to end on one real, public-safe slice of FlyRank's own data.

Features used throughout: average search position, total impressions, total clicks, and click-through rate, each aggregated per page over a single calendar month. All are knowable at the decision moment — none depend on data from after the month being scored. The sealed final month of the release (June 2026) was never used to build or tune anything; it was held out entirely, per the release's own guidance.

Excluded by design: raw query text, raw URLs, and any future-window metric that could leak the outcome into the features.

03Methodology

The baseline

A hand-written rule flags pages sitting at search positions 11–20 (just off page one) with at least 100 impressions in the month, plus a secondary flag for well-ranked pages (top 10) with unusually low click-through rate. This is the standard a learned model has to beat.

The label

A page is labeled declining if its clicks the following month fall below 80% of its clicks this month, among pages with at least 100 impressions. This is a proxy, not a verified cause of decline — a page can lose clicks for reasons a refresh won't fix, such as seasonality.

Known artifact in this label: a page with zero clicks in the feature month can never be labeled declining, since no amount of further drop is possible. 39.6% of feature-month pages fall into this zero-click group. Any comparison between methods has to account for this, or it will look more decisive than it is.

The model

A shallow decision tree (max depth 4, class-balanced), chosen over a forest or boosted model because the goal this early is an interpretable result that can be read and questioned, not the highest possible score.

Validation design

Two weaknesses had to be closed before any result was trustworthy. First, evaluation is time-aware: the model trains on January–February features and is scored on April–May outcomes, never on data from its own training window. Second, evaluation is grouped by client: 30% of clients are held out of training entirely, so the test set contains only clients the model has never seen. Both were necessary — page overlap between the training and test months alone accounted for 52,792 shared pages before grouping was introduced.

Where the two methods actually disagree

The most useful finding in this project may be this one: the tree's top 500 ranked pages and the rule's top 500 ranked pages share zero pages in common. The rule clusters tightly around position 11, the edge of its defined "quick-win zone." The tree spreads its picks across positions 11–35, favoring somewhat lower impressions and marginally higher click-through rate. The two methods are not refining the same shortlist — they are finding different populations entirely.

05Limitations & honest framing

  • Small client panel. All numbers above come from 50 clients, 15 held out per draw. This is enough to be directional, not enough to be a stable, production-grade benchmark.
  • Result instability. The tree's P@50 ranged from 0.26 to 0.80 depending on which clients were held out. Reporting only the mean would overstate how reliable a single run is.
  • Label artifact. 39.6% of pages start with zero clicks and are permanently ineligible to be labeled declining under this formula, which shapes what the model can ever learn to flag.
  • No causal claim. Nothing here shows that any signal causes decline, or that refreshing a flagged page would recover clicks. The evidence is observational and directional.

Claims in this paper are deliberately scoped to what was measured: observed patterns in this dataset, directional relationships, and decision-support for a human reviewer — never proof about how any search engine ranks pages.

06Ranked recommendations

Because the two methods flag different pages with no overlap, the playbook treats them as two separate, lower-confidence signals rather than blending them into one score.

Reason codeMeaningSuggested action
rule_onlyNear page-one edge, real impressionsReview for refresh
tree_onlyLearned pattern flags it; use with more cautionReview for refresh
neither_flagNo signal from either methodNo action

Never automate

  • Auto-publishing content changes based on a score alone.
  • Auto-deprioritizing or removing a page based on a score alone.
  • Treating a tree_only pick as equal-confidence to a manually reviewed finding, given its 0.26–0.80 range.
  • Applying this queue to clients outside the 50 evaluated here without re-validating first.

Monitor and retrain when

  • Precision@50 on a fresh held-out month drops well below the 0.60 average observed here.
  • The zero-overlap pattern between the two methods changes materially.
  • More than roughly 90 days pass since the last retrain.
